Selina Xiao

Aug 30, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

For the Poisoning Attack on LLMs paper, it is counterintuitive that attack success appears to depend much more strongly on the absolute number of poisoned samples than on their fraction of the training set. I also found it interesting that the same qualitative behavior appears across different model sizes and both pretraining and fine-tuning settings. For the Robustness Meets Algorithms paper, I found it interesting that the main contribution is not simply using eigenvectors, but proving that if adversarial samples significantly distort the estimate, they must leave a detectable spectral signature. This turns a robustness problem into something algorithmically testable, and the same insight can be used both for filtering and for a convex feasibility formulation.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

For the Poisoning Attack on LLMs paper, I am curious whether there is a theoretical or mechanistic explanation for why the absolute number of poisoned samples, rather than the poisoning ratio, determines attack success. The paper provides extensive empirical evidence and suggests that the number of gradient updates involving poisoned data may matter, but it is still unclear to me why much larger amounts of clean data do not dilute the learned backdoor. I would also like to better understand how these results change for more complex attacks than the relatively simple trigger-response behaviors considered here. For the Robustness Meets Algorithms paper, I'm curious about how much of the spectral filtering idea survives when the clean distribution is only approximately Gaussian or has heavier tails? In particular, could ordinary model misspecification create the same moment abnormalities that the algorithm interprets as corruption?

Aditya Kotha

Sep 1, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Paper 1: Given the chinchilla laws on how much data is needed to train large LLMs, this paper makes a very good argument that simply poisoning a constant number of samples spread across the training set gives attackers various exploits to use. Previous works mainly show that they require a percentage of the training data to work, but for large-scale models, this percentage amounts to a very large amount of data that must be poisoned, which is impractical. Another interesting observation is that it's very hard to distinguish the trained checkpoints using traditional evaluations alone, as the performance difference between the poisoned model and the normal SFT-trained model is nearly identical in their results, making this even harder to detect during the normal LLM training and eval pipeline. And another observation that makes this interesting is that these attacks are easier with pretrained-only models, but in the paper itself, they haven’t run ablations to see whether running some safety finetuning or post-training would still make these attacks transfer? Paper 2: It's actually a very interesting paper that builds on the hypothesis that if a malicious actor has added in samples to move your estimate a lot, they must leave some sort of detectable statistical fingerprint that can be identified. The easiest way to find this, apparently, is to compute the turkey median, but this becomes challenging in higher dimensions. The paper mentions they haven’t seen any cases of people doing this above a 6-dimensional, moderately sized dataset, as it's an NP-hard problem to compute these robust estimates. So it's very interesting how the paper tries to solve this by assuming that if an attacker adds points in a direction that moves the estimate, they must also increase the variance in that direction. Now it becomes easy to calculate the top eigenvalues/vectors in that direction and remove the points that cause the bulging of the sphere to happen in the Gaussian distribution.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Paper 1: If the DoS attack is simply a random sampling of tokens sampled from the vocab, wouldn’t the preprocessing pipelines catch it? (like dedup checks) Can this actually be used as a kind of watermark for protected IPs, so that models trained on their data without permission can be easily identified? Figure 4 shows that the absolute poison count remains the dominant factor, but higher poison density within individual batches appears to require more poisoned examples. Could the more fundamental quantity actually be the number of gradient updates that contain poisoned data rather than just the raw number of poisoned samples? Paper 2: Could we simply use PCA to reduce dimensionality before robustness estimation, so we can use the median approach? Or would these corrupted samples also affect the features after PCA, and would it become harder to detect? How would these robust estimations look like if the distribution is different than a Gaussian? Discussion: I would like to learn a bit more about the feasibility of these attacks on a large scale. For example, if I create 250 webpages on the internet and LLM providers scrape this data to train their models, does that even seem feasible? I would like to learn more about how to use these robust statistical techniques in real-life machine learning. Would outlier removal and other techniques be considered to do the same, etc.?

One thing I found interesting in the poisoning paper is that it challenged my original intuition about data contamination. For Robustness Meets Algorithms, I liked the contrast between the simple one-dimensional case and the much harder high-dimensional case. In one dimension, using the median gives a natural way to be robust to outliers, but in high dimensions, methods that are robust may become computationally difficult, while easy methods may lose accuracy. This tradeoff felt very familiar from an operations research perspective. In the experiment, we may assume that the data follow some distribution P, but in reality the data are usually only approximately generated from P, and some observations may even be corrupted. The important question is whether the method can still return a reliable answer when the assumptions are not exactly true. This connects strongly to how I think about robust optimization in OR, where we also try to make decisions that remain good when the nominal assumptions are slightly wrong.
