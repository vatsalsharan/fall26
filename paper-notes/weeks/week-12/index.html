What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Learning with Privacy at Scale: I found the keyboard example (about words missing their final letter) interesting, since it actually also points to a usability problem. I know this is kind of a side thought from the main idea of the paper, but it made me wonder what information we actually need to improve a product and how this kind of analysis could work alongside user research to understand why a pattern might be happening. Semi-Supervised Knowledge Transfer: I found the teacher-student setup interesting after I understood how the data gets divided. I initially didn't get why each teacher would get less data as more teachers were added, but then it made sense after I realized that it's a set amount of data and their private training examples don't overlap so it's the same amount of data being split among more teachers. I also found it interesting how having more teachers isn't necessarily better since each one would have less data to learn from, and this might even cause their performance to worsen.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Learning with Privacy at Scale: If the system focuses on popular patterns, could it miss problems affecting smaller groups (for example, people who use less common dialects)? How could we investigate those needs while still protecting privacy? How is privacy tracked over time? I know the paper talks about protection for individual events and limits on daily reports, but similarly to my questions from the Renyi paper, what happens when someone contributes reports for months or years? I hope to learn how these limits are chosen and see what we can learn about different users. Semi-Supervised Knowledge Transfer: How would someone decide how many teachers to use for a new task? How do we know when each teacher has too little information to learn well? I'm curious to learn how this choice might depend on the amount of data and the difficulty of the task, or any other potential factors.

Alan Liang

Oct 16, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found it interesting that Apple could discover new words without starting with a complete dictionary. Sequence Fragment Puzzle combines popular privatized fragments into candidate words and then estimates their frequencies. I also found the “lov” and “kno” example interesting: these shortened words helped reveal that people were accidentally tapping a keyboard prediction before finishing the word. This showed how aggregate statistics could uncover a specific interface problem. I found it interesting that PATE provides privacy even though the teachers themselves are trained without differential privacy. The protection comes from noisy aggregation of their votes, and the student retains its privacy guarantee even if its weights are public. I was also surprised that the student reached 98% accuracy on MNIST with only 100 teacher-label queries, using semi-supervised learning on a larger pool of public images.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

How does privacy loss accumulate when the same person contributes reports every day, given that the guarantee is per event? How reliable are the estimates for small language communities or rare words after adding noise and applying frequency thresholds? I hope to see a simple example showing how Hadamard Count Mean Sketch can transmit one privatized bit plus index information while keeping the frequency estimates reasonably accurate. How should the number of teachers be chosen when adding more teachers leaves less training data for each one? What happens to rare classes that only a few teachers learn well? I hope to see a simple example explaining why strong teacher agreement lowers the privacy cost. I also want to understand how much the student’s performance depends on having public unlabeled data that resemble the private data.

Yuyang Jiang

Oct 19, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found the different approaches to privacy-preserving learning particularly interesting. The PATE paper shows how knowledge can be transferred from multiple models trained on disjoint private datasets to a student model through noisy aggregation, without directly exposing the underlying training data. Apple's work demonstrates how local differential privacy can be deployed at a massive scale by randomizing user data before it leaves individual devices. I especially liked how the two approaches place privacy protection at different stages of the learning pipeline. Together, they highlight that privacy-preserving machine learning is not just about adding noise, but also about designing the overall system architecture to balance privacy, utility, and scalability.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I had is how we should decide where to introduce privacy protection in a machine learning pipeline. PATE protects private training data through noisy teacher aggregation, whereas Apple's approach privatizes individual records before they reach the server. These methods have different assumptions about data access, trust, and computational resources. I wonder how much the choice between these architectures affects the achievable privacy-utility trade-off, especially when moving from relatively simple classification or frequency estimation tasks to more complex foundation model training. I would be interested in discussing whether there are general principles for choosing the most appropriate privacy mechanism for different real-world applications.

Oct 19, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found it interesting that the two papers use differential privacy at very different points in the learning pipeline. PATE partitions sensitive data across multiple teacher models and trains a student only from their noisy aggregated predictions, so the final student never directly accesses the private training data. In contrast, Apple’s system applies local differential privacy directly on the user’s device, so the server never receives the raw data at all. The system also removes identifiers, discards IP addresses, and separates records from different use cases before aggregation. learning-with-privacy-at-scale. I thought this contrast was especially interesting because both systems provide formal privacy guarantees, but they make very different assumptions about who is trusted and where privacy protection should happen.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I have is how we should choose between local differential privacy and approaches such as PATE in a real application. I hope to learn more about how theoretical privacy guarantees translate into engineering tradeoffs at scale.
