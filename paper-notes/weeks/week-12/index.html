What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found the different approaches to privacy-preserving learning particularly interesting. The PATE paper shows how knowledge can be transferred from multiple models trained on disjoint private datasets to a student model through noisy aggregation, without directly exposing the underlying training data. Apple's work demonstrates how local differential privacy can be deployed at a massive scale by randomizing user data before it leaves individual devices. I especially liked how the two approaches place privacy protection at different stages of the learning pipeline. Together, they highlight that privacy-preserving machine learning is not just about adding noise, but also about designing the overall system architecture to balance privacy, utility, and scalability.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I had is how we should decide where to introduce privacy protection in a machine learning pipeline. PATE protects private training data through noisy teacher aggregation, whereas Apple's approach privatizes individual records before they reach the server. These methods have different assumptions about data access, trust, and computational resources. I wonder how much the choice between these architectures affects the achievable privacy-utility trade-off, especially when moving from relatively simple classification or frequency estimation tasks to more complex foundation model training. I would be interested in discussing whether there are general principles for choosing the most appropriate privacy mechanism for different real-world applications.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Learning with Privacy at Scale paper It's a very interesting approach, different from what I was generally thinking about when adding DP in practice: adding noise to the results afterward or during training itself, as we were learning in the past few papers. Instead, local DP adds the randomization even before the data is sent to the server or here at Apple. Although noise is added, when aggregated across all the users they collect data on, it's still possible to extract meaningful information and patterns. Another surprising point was that the Hadamard Count Mean Sketch required only one bit of privatized data per report, along with the indexes needed for interpretation, making the communication process extremely inexpensive. I also liked how the Sequence Fragment Puzzle can discover new words not already in a dictionary by combining protected information about word fragments, and it also showed that this method was practical, as it was able to find slang words like “bruh” and “tryna”. SEMI-SUPERVISED KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER paper It's interesting that teacher models trained on a disjoint set of private data can help create a student model via a noisy voting mechanism that provides formal differential privacy. The point of dividing the private data into n disjoint sets is that changing an individual's data will affect only one teacher model's decision. Since this is a voting-based ensemble, the final output will be very little affected, as it can change at most one vote on a given query. Adding Laplacian noise also helps hide private information from the final results voting process. Another very interesting point is that when there is strong agreement among the votes, this reduces the privacy cost because the result reflects agreement across many teachers rather than relying heavily on any one teacher’s private training data. They also achieve good results on the MNIST dataset by running 100 queries on the teacher ensemble model and using publicly available unlabeled data to train the student. They achieved 98% accuracy with epsilon 2.04 and delta 0.00001.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Learning with Privacy at Scale paper I wonder how well these mechanisms would work if a new pattern appeared in only a few reports. For example, would Sequence Fragment Puzzle still be able to reconstruct a rare word, or would the noise and frequency thresholds prevent it from being detected? SEMI-SUPERVISED KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER paper Does the way we create the disjoint training sets for the PATE method affect its performance? For example, instead of just randomly splitting the data, what if I try to balance the output class labels to ensure they are equal, so that the teacher models can learn better? Would it affect the privacy guarantee in any way? I also wonder how well a model trained with PATE would perform when the publicly available unlabeled data differs substantially from the private training data, especially for specialized tasks where suitable public data is not readily available. I hope to learn a bit more about local DP and whether there are other methods not covered in the papers that help facilitate it.

Nana Xia

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Learning with privacy at scale from Apple didn't wait until model training or model release to add privacy. Instead, it applied local differential privacy on the user's device before the data were sent to Apple server. Then the server didn't need to have the raw user event at all and it could build a very trustworthy model. The paper also introduced new method called sequence fragment puzzle to discover new words that were not in the known dictionary and it was able to privately collect fragments and reconstructed likely popular words. The paper of semi-supervised knowledge transfer for deep learning from private training data brought the idea of "teacher-student" framework. Instead of using the teachers trained from the raw data, it used the fixed amount of teachers and semi-supervised learning method (e.g., GANs) to train the students which helped the privacy loss. What stood out is the PATE model does not depend strongly on the specific learning algorithm used for the teachers or the student. The paper shows that it can be applied not only to deep neural networks on MNIST and SVHN, but also to other model structures, including random forests on medical data.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I have is about the data-dependent privacy accounting in PATE. The paper shows that a teacher query can have a lower privacy cost when the teachers strongly agree. For example, if almost all teachers vote for the same class, changing the prediction of one teacher is unlikely to change the final noisy output. In contrast, when the teacher votes are very close, changing one vote may have a much larger effect. The moments-accountant analysis therefore gives a tighter privacy bound when the top class has a large vote margin. So how much this strong teacher agreement contributes to the overall privacy guarantee, and what happens when many queries have weak teacher agreement?
