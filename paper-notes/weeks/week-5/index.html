What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I find it especially interesting that fairness in recommender systems cannot really be treated as a fixed offline evaluation problem. The practitioners have to deploy the system first, observe how users actually interact with it, and then discover new sources of unfairness that were not visible from historical data alone. I also found it interesting that, despite the large academic literature on fairness metrics, practitioners still rely heavily on intuition and develop their own system-specific metrics in practice. This stood out to me because it suggests that the gap between fairness theory and deployed systems is not only about finding better metrics, but also about understanding how those metrics behave once they interact with a changing real-world system.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

How should companies safely experiment with fairness interventions when the only reliable way to observe some harms is through real-world deployment? Could simulation, causal models, or carefully controlled online experiments reduce this dependence on “deploy first, discover later”? Also, if fairness metrics themselves are repeatedly revised after deployment, is there a principled way to distinguish between adapting the metric because the environment changed and simply changing the definition of fairness until the system looks acceptable?

Yuyang Jiang

Sep 18, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found the distinction between learning and steering in performative prediction particularly interesting. Traditional machine learning treats the data-generating distribution as something external that the model learns from, whereas in performative settings, deploying a model can itself change that distribution. This means that a model can achieve lower risk either by learning the existing distribution better or by steering the population toward a distribution that is easier or more favorable for the model to predict. I found this perspective especially interesting because it changes how we think about model evaluation: good predictive performance after deployment does not necessarily mean that the model accurately learned a pre-existing environment, since part of that performance may result from the model changing the environment itself.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I had is whether reaching a performatively stable point should actually be considered a desirable outcome. A model can become stable because the population has adapted to it, meaning that the data observed after deployment no longer provides evidence that the model should change. But this does not necessarily imply that the resulting equilibrium is socially desirable or even globally optimal. This makes me wonder how we should evaluate performative systems beyond convergence or predictive risk. In particular, what criteria should we use to distinguish between a model that successfully learns from a changing population and one that mainly succeeds by steering the population toward an equilibrium favorable to its own objective?

For the "Goal Misgeneralization in Deep RL" paper, the finding I found most interesting is in Section 3.4. The actor and the critic are both learned, and they end up with different proxies. The critic assigns highest value to states where the agent touches the end wall, but when the wall is made permeable the actor walks through it every time. For the "fairness in the workflow" paper, what I found most interesting is the communication gap with fairness teams. Nana Xia

Sep 18, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I appreciate that Fairness-in-the-Workflow focuses on the more fundamental questions of what fairness means and whose fairness matters. These questions are not as straightforward as simply developing or optimizing a model, and in some sense they come before the optimization problem itself. Different stakeholders—such as end users, content providers, advertisers, technical teams, and fairness teams—may have different ideas about what a fair recommender system should look like, so there is no single definition of fairness that can be applied everywhere. I also found the dynamic nature of fairness especially interesting. Even when fairness is considered during model development, practitioners often discover new fairness issues only after real users begin interacting with the deployed system. Because user behavior and feedback can further influence future recommendations, a system that appears fair today may not remain fair over time. This made me think that fairness should be viewed as an ongoing process rather than a one-time metric or requirement.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I have is about when and how fairness should be integrated into the workflow. The paper finds that legal teams are involved relatively early and communication with them is often easier because legal requirements are more explicit about what is allowed and what is not. In contrast, fairness teams often become involved later, when the model has already been developed or even deployed, so making major changes at that stage can be much more costly. The authors therefore suggest integrating fairness earlier and more frequently throughout the workflow. However, the paper also shows that fairness is dynamic: even if a model passes fairness checks before deployment, new problems may appear as users interact with the system and feedback loops develop. This makes me wonder what a practical continuous fairness-monitoring workflow should look like. How often should fairness be re-evaluated, when should fairness metrics be updated, and how can we distinguish a real new fairness problem from changes caused by user behavior or previous recommendations? I think the more interesting question is how fairness can be built into the model lifecycle as an ongoing process, rather than treated as a one-time check before deployment.
