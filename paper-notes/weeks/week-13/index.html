Aditya Kotha

Oct 22, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

SEMI-SUPERVISED KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER paper It's interesting that teacher models trained on a disjoint set of private data can help create a student model via a noisy voting mechanism that provides formal differential privacy. The point of dividing the private data into n disjoint sets is that changing an individual's data will affect only one teacher model's decision. Since this is a voting-based ensemble, the final output will be very little affected, as it can change at most one vote on a given query. Adding Laplacian noise also helps hide private information from the final results voting process. Another very interesting point is that when there is strong agreement among the votes, this reduces the privacy cost because the result reflects agreement across many teachers rather than relying heavily on any one teacher’s private training data. They also achieve good results on the MNIST dataset by running 100 queries on the teacher ensemble model and using publicly available unlabeled data to train the student. They achieved 98% accuracy with epsilon 2.04 and delta 0.00001.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

SEMI-SUPERVISED KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER paper Does the way we create the disjoint training sets for the PATE method affect its performance? For example, instead of just randomly splitting the data, what if I try to balance the output class labels to ensure they are equal, so that the teacher models can learn better? Would it affect the privacy guarantee in any way? I also wonder how well a model trained with PATE would perform when the publicly available unlabeled data differs substantially from the private training data, especially for specialized tasks where suitable public data is not readily available.

Sreyes Venkatesh

Oct 23, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

One particular thing I found interesting was the findings of the approaches that did not reduce the number of queries: Distillation gave gains too small to justify releasing full probability vectors instead of plurality votes; Siamese networks for active learning didn’t generalize. Binary expert classifiers gave 1.5 to 2.5%; What actually worked was the seemingly most simple method. i.e. let the student rank unlabeled inputs by its own low confidence and spend queries there first, for 4 to 10% gains at fixed budget. Another point of interest to me was the construction of the method, the authors partition the sensitive data into n disjoint sets, train an ordinary teacher on each, have them vote on labels for public unlabeled data with Laplace noise on the vote counts, train a student on those noisy labels, deploy only the student. Note that none of the teachers are privacy aware. There’s also no modified optimizer, no gradient clipping, no per example bookkeeping inside training. This guarantee comes from the topology of the pipeline rather than from the learning algorithm and interests me in particular because it demonstrates success on non convex functions like neural nets.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

A question I had about the Semi Supervised Knowledge transfer paper was given that the student is a distillation of the teachers, and the teachers memorize, I was wondering what would happen if membership inference attacks against PATE students at epsilon = 8 and if that empirical leakage would be consistent with the formal bound. Also the authors admit that ensembling gets impractical when the number of classes is large. For something like a thousand class problem or a rare disease medical dataset, I cannot imagine the partitions can be both clean and well populated. I was wondering at what classes per partition ratio does the ensemble stop being confident enough to tolerate noise?

Yuyang Jiang

Oct 23, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found the idea of transferring knowledge from private models to a public student model particularly interesting. Rather than directly applying differential privacy to the entire training process, PATE trains multiple teachers on disjoint subsets of private data and uses noisy aggregation of their predictions to supervise a student model. I especially liked the intuition that agreement among teachers can help reduce privacy costs, since the aggregated prediction becomes less dependent on any individual teacher’s training data. More broadly, the paper demonstrates how designing the learning architecture itself can create opportunities for stronger privacy guarantees without requiring major restrictions on how individual models are trained.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I had is how the effectiveness of PATE depends on the degree of agreement among teachers. The framework benefits from strong consensus, but in more complex tasks, teachers trained on disjoint datasets may learn substantially different decision boundaries or exhibit systematic disagreements. How would this affect the privacy-utility trade-off, especially when disagreement reflects meaningful differences in the underlying data rather than random noise? I would also be interested in discussing whether PATE can scale effectively to modern foundation models, where training multiple independent teachers may be computationally expensive and transferring knowledge through discrete predictions may be insufficient.

Ke Xu

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I find it interesting how the paper takes local differential privacy from a theoretical concept to a system deployed across hundreds of millions of devices. Instead of collecting raw user data and adding noise afterward, Apple randomizes each record directly on the user's device, so the server never receives the original information. I also like that privacy is not achieved through differential privacy alone, but through several additional design choices, such as limiting the number of reports, removing identifiers, and separating records from different use cases. Another interesting part is the trade-off between privacy, estimation accuracy, communication cost, and server computation. In particular, the Hadamard Count Mean Sketch algorithm allows a device to transmit only a single privatized bit while still enabling the server to estimate frequencies across a large population. Sorry for the confusion, I didn't notice that the papers for Oct 21 and Oct 26 had been switched, so I accidentally submitted my responses under the opposite dates.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

I'm curious about how the authors choose the privacy parameter and other algorithmic parameters in practice. The paper provides theoretical bounds on estimation variance, but I wonder how sensitive the results are to the underlying data distribution, especially when some items are rare. For example, if a word or emoji is used by only a small number of people, how reliably can the system distinguish its true frequency from the noise introduced for privacy? I'm also interested in how the privacy guarantees behave when users contribute data repeatedly over time. The system limits the number of reports per day and removes identifiers, but does that provide sufficient protection when the same user participates over a long period? I'd like to better understand how the event-level privacy guarantees relate to user-level privacy, and how the system balances long-term privacy protection with the need to collect useful statistics.
