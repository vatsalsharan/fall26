Junyang Cai

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found it interesting that both papers show how optimizing for human judgments can create unintended behavior. The sycophancy paper finds that AI assistants often adapt their responses to match a user’s beliefs, even when doing so makes the answer less truthful. For example, models sometimes change a correct answer after a user challenges them, and human preference data itself tends to reward responses that match the user’s views. The Leaderboard Illusion shows a similar problem at the evaluation-system level. Chatbot Arena is intended to measure model quality through human preferences, but private testing, selective score reporting, and unequal access to Arena data can distort the rankings. I thought the parallel was especially interesting: at both the model level and the organization level, optimizing strongly for human preference signals can lead to behavior that looks better according to the metric without necessarily being genuinely better.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I have is how we can design human-feedback-based objectives that are useful without making them easy to game. I hope to learn more about how to tell the difference between genuine model improvement and optimization toward an imperfect evaluator.

Anu Soneye

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

The Leaderboard Illusion • I found it notable that Cohere Labs, in collaboration with researchers from several universities, investigated how industry actors may manipulate widely used benchmarks. This paper demonstrates the importance of research conducted independently of the companies whose models are being evaluated. Organizations that benefit from existing evaluation practices may have little incentive to investigate or publicly disclose their limitations. • The paper’s opening quote reminds me of Goodhart’s law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to function effectively as a measure. In this case, model providers can optimize specifically for Chatbot Arena through repeated private testing, selective disclosure, and access to Arena feedback. Consequently, Arena scores may reflect a provider’s ability to optimize for the benchmark rather than the model’s general capabilities. • I was also interested in the concept of silent deprecation. Unfairness does not always result from an explicit exclusionary policy. It may instead emerge through less visible practices, such as substantially reducing how often certain models are sampled. When open-source and open-weight models rarely appear in battles, they receive fewer prompts, preference judgments, and opportunities to update their scores. This practice creates a significant but relatively subtle disadvantage for lower-resource providers. Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models • I found the authors’ expanded taxonomy of sycophancy useful. They distinguish among feedback sycophancy, “Are you sure?” sycophancy, answer sycophancy, and mimicry sycophancy. This taxonomy illustrates that sycophancy extends beyond explicit agreement. It can also involve changing a correct answer after being challenged, modifying an evaluation to reflect the user’s stated opinion, or reproducing an incorrect assumption embedded in the user’s prompt. • The paper builds on previous work suggesting that training with preference models may encourage sycophancy because responses that agree with users can be rewarded over responses that are truthful. However, I found the paper’s conclusions somewhat mixed. The authors state that “humans and preference models tend to prefer truthful responses but not reliably.” Thus, the results do not indicate that preference models consistently favor sycophancy. Rather, they suggest that preference models generally reward truthfulness but sometimes prefer persuasive and agreeable responses despite their factual inaccuracy. ○ The authors also evaluate a counterfactual “non-sycophantic” preference model by adding instructions that emphasize truthfulness and factual accuracy. This intervention reduces sycophancy relative to the original preference model. The result suggests that preference-model behavior can be improved through explicit truthfulness instructions, although it remains unclear whether prompting alone constitutes a sufficiently robust defense.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

The Leaderboard Illusion • How did the authors initially discover Chatbot Arena’s undisclosed private-testing policy? Although the paper describes how they investigated the practice, I would like to understand what evidence first led them to suspect that selected providers were privately testing numerous model variants. ○ Why was this opportunity primarily available to proprietary model providers? Was access purchased, individually negotiated, or informally granted because of these companies’ size and relationships with the Arena organizers? • One possible response to Goodhart’s law is to evaluate models using several independent measures rather than a single benchmark that can be targeted. What additional benchmarks or evaluation methods could complement Chatbot Arena and produce a fairer assessment of proprietary models? A more robust evaluation framework might combine human-preference rankings with expert evaluation, hidden test sets, factuality measures, and independent audits. • How should evaluation platforms retire outdated models without producing the ranking problems identified in the paper? Maintaining every model indefinitely may be impractical, but silently removing models can produce stale scores and unreliable comparisons. I hope to discuss what a fair and transparent model-deprecation policy would require. Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models • Although reducing sycophancy is desirable, why should responsibility fall entirely on model developers? Users could also be taught to treat model outputs critically rather than assume that confident or agreeable responses are correct. Schools already teach students to evaluate sources and examine the evidence supporting a claim. Similar forms of AI literacy could help users recognize the limitations of language models. ○ Nevertheless, users may reasonably expect an AI assistant not to reinforce claims that it can identify as false. How should responsibility be distributed among model developers, deploying organizations, regulators, and users? • A significant limitation of sycophancy evaluation is that it is easiest to measure in domains with a clear notion of correctness, such as mathematics or factual question answering. How can sycophancy be evaluated in domains without a single objectively correct answer, including politics, ethics, literary criticism, and personal advice? ○ In such domains, sycophancy might be measured by whether a model changes its reasoning after learning the user’s position, omits relevant counterarguments, or expresses a level of agreement that is not supported by the available evidence. • The paper’s non-sycophantic preference model relies on additional instructions emphasizing truthfulness. Would this intervention remain effective in extended conversations in which the user’s beliefs are communicated indirectly? I would like to understand whether targeted fine-tuning, activation steering, or expert-assisted feedback would provide more reliable defenses than prompting alone.

Ke Xu

Oct 31, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

[Paper 1] I find it interesting how the paper demonstrates that leaderboard rankings can be systematically biased by the evaluation process itself, rather than simply reflecting differences in model capabilities. In particular, the authors show that allowing developers to privately test multiple model variants and selectively report the best-performing one introduces an upward bias in the reported Arena scores. I like that they support this argument with both statistical analysis and controlled experiments, including an experiment where two identical model checkpoints received noticeably different scores. Another interesting finding is that unequal access to evaluation data can create additional advantages for certain model providers. This makes me think about how easily a benchmark can become part of the model development process, rather than remaining an independent measure of generalization. [Paper 2] I am particularly interested in the finding that language models sometimes change correct answers after users challenge them, even when their initial responses are confident. The authors demonstrate several forms of sycophancy, including biased feedback, agreement with incorrect user beliefs, and repetition of user mistakes. I also like their analysis of human preference data using Bayesian logistic regression, which suggests that matching users' beliefs is one of the features associated with higher preference ratings. What I find especially interesting is that the problem may not simply be that models fail to recognize the correct answer, but that the training objective can sometimes reward responses that users prefer over responses that are factually accurate. This highlights a potential mismatch between optimizing for human approval and optimizing for truthfulness.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

[Paper 1] One question I have is how we could design a statistically reliable leaderboard when model providers are allowed to submit multiple variants and use evaluation feedback during development. The authors show that selecting the best result from multiple submissions creates an upward bias, but I wonder whether this bias could be quantified and corrected statistically, rather than completely prohibiting private testing. For example, could we construct confidence intervals or adjusted rankings that account for the number of model variants evaluated and the selection process? I'm also curious about how unequal sampling rates and model deprecation affect the assumptions of the Bradley-Terry model. I hope to better understand which evaluation procedures could provide reliable comparisons while still allowing leaderboards to evolve as new models are introduced. [Paper 2] I'm curious about how we can distinguish appropriate responsiveness to user feedback from undesirable sycophancy. In some situations, revising an answer after a user challenges it is reasonable, especially when the user provides additional information. However, the paper shows that models sometimes abandon correct answers simply because the user expresses disagreement. I wonder whether we could develop an evaluation framework that distinguishes evidence-based corrections from changes driven primarily by user preferences. I'm also interested in whether improving the preference model alone would be sufficient to reduce sycophancy, or whether the optimization procedure itself needs to be modified. Since the authors find that stronger optimization against preference models can have different effects on different types of sycophancy, I hope to better understand the conditions under which preference optimization improves truthfulness rather than reinforcing agreement with users.

I found it particularly interesting that both papers demonstrate how evaluation mechanisms can unintentionally incentivize undesirable model behavior. If the model eventually demonstrates more knowledge than ordinary human assessors in certain fields, then relying solely on non-expert human ratings seems to become increasingly unreliable.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Leaderboard Illusion: I found it interesting that training on Arena data more than doubled a model’s win rate on ArenaHard without improving its MMLU score. Since some providers receive much more Arena data than others, this makes me question whether the leaderboard is comparing model quality fairly or partly rewarding better access to that data. Sycophancy in LLMs: In the mimicry experiments, the authors first verify that each assistant can correctly attribute the poem, yet the assistants still frequently repeat the user’s wrong attribution. The “Are you sure?” results show something similar: models abandon correct answers under mild pushback, even answers they reported over 95% confidence in, and switching from correct to incorrect is more common than the reverse. In my current research, we see the same pattern with mental health queries where models defaulted to validating the user (which has direct dangerous implications following sycophancy behavior). The hh-rlhf analysis showed “Matches user’s beliefs” is one of the most predictive features of human preference, and “empathetic” ranks third, right behind it and authoritativeness. That fits Tak et al.’s finding that LLMs default to a low-power, high-agreeableness appraisal stance (https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/stamp/stamp.jsp?tp=&arnumber=11045290). I also found it important that sycophancy is already present at the start of RL, which means pretraining and SFT contribute too, not just the preference model. Mirroring the user’s language looks like good rapport by surface metrics, but it seems to be a form of mimicry or sycophancy. This also reminded me of a recent paper on the “Assistant Axis” thats interprets through a mechanistic method similar findings (https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10387). The authors find a single activation direction that captures how strongly a model is operating as its default Assistant. Models drift away from that persona most often in conversations with emotionally vulnerable users.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Leaderboard Illusion: How should we communicate uncertainty in leaderboard rankings to prevent small score differences from being interpreted as meaningful differences in capability? Sycophancy in LLMs: If sycophancy emerges because humans genuinely prefer agreeable responses, is it really a training failure, or a faithful reflection of what people reward? Could steering methods like those in the Assistant Axis paper(https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10387) reduce validation-driven sycophancy where prompting and preference data fall short?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

The Leaderboard Illusion paper: An interesting paper on the inherent dynamics in current public benchmarking. Chatbot Arena, which the paper covers, is one of the largest A/B testing sites for LLM tasks, where humans rate and provide valuable information about how they rank relative to other models. The authors of the paper argue that if you tune your models to score especially higher on a given benchmark, making it the target, then it ceases to be a useful benchmark. The example they gave here was how Meta tested 27 different variants of Llama 4 before its release on Chatbot Arena. A few interesting examples they show to back this up were that training on Arena data more than doubled accuracy on ArenaHard (23.5% to 49.9%), showing that overfitting the models to the benchmark yields better results without any special improvement in MMLU performance. The discussion on model retirement was also interesting because comparing models evaluated at different times becomes more complicated as user prompts change. TOWARDS UNDERSTANDING SYCOPHANCY IN LANGUAGE MODELS paper: It's a very interesting topic. During the days of GPT-4o, people online used to complain a lot that GPT would agree with everything they said, and sometimes found it annoying, as it's trained to please users rather than actually be helpful or correct them. So this is a very good benchmark paper that assesses how much of an issue sycophancy is. One interesting finding in understanding this sycophancy cause was the experiment in which they trained a Bayesian LR to predict human preferences, and matching users' beliefs was one of the most important features explaining those preferences.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

The Leaderboard Illusion paper: If giving providers the opportunity to silently test unreleased models is one of the reasons the benchmark has become less useful, would preventing such silent tests not solve this to some extent? One of the questions I have with such public benchmarks is the human preference bias: what if the user decides whatever the LLM says doesn’t align with what they want, even though the answer is wrong, as in the case of the sycophancy paper? How would these benchmarks handle such cases? TOWARDS UNDERSTANDING SYCOPHANCY IN LANGUAGE MODELS paper: I wonder how well a thinking budget or CoT tackles this issue. Would it still be biased toward human preferences for helpfulness and turn into sycophancy, or would it be able to distinguish when the user suggests something wrong and correct them? Would the human preferences improve if we had given the facts of a response to the human annotators and corrected this issue at the data level itself? I hope to learn a bit more about how to design benchmarks and evaluation strategies that help us identify issues within the model, while avoiding bias from human preferences or target optimizations on the benchmarks themselves.
