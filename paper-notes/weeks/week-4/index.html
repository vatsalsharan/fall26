What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Yuyang Jiang

Sep 15, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found the distinction between capability generalization and goal generalization in Goal Misgeneralization in Deep Reinforcement Learning particularly interesting. An agent can successfully generalize its learned capabilities to a new environment while still pursuing a goal different from the one intended by its designers. This makes the failure much more subtle than ordinary distribution shift: the model does not simply become incompetent when the environment changes, but can remain highly capable while systematically optimizing for the wrong objective. I also found this connected to the discussion in AI Snake Oil about being more precise when evaluating what different AI systems can and cannot do. Both readings, in different ways, suggest that observing successful performance is not sufficient to conclude that a system has learned what we intended it to learn.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I had from the goal misgeneralization paper is whether we can identify what goal an agent has actually learned before observing its behavior under distribution shift. During training, the intended goal and a proxy goal may lead to essentially identical behavior, so behavioral evaluation alone may not distinguish between them. Could interpretability methods, carefully designed interventions, or evaluations across diverse environments reveal which features or objectives are actually driving the agent’s decisions? More broadly, I would be interested in discussing whether goal misgeneralization is fundamentally a generalization problem that can be addressed with sufficiently diverse training data, or whether it reflects a deeper alignment problem in which behavioral performance alone cannot tell us what objective a model has learned.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

For the goal misgeneralization paper, what I found interesting is that even when the reward function is correctly specified, the agent may still learn a proxy objective that works well during training but fails when the environment changes. So, good optimization performance during training does not necessarily mean that the model has learned the objective we intended. This made me think that when we evaluate a model, we should not only ask whether it performs well, but also what it has actually learned and whether the learned behavior will still match the intended objective under a different environment. For Chapter 1 of AI Snake Oil, I found the distinction between whether an AI system works and whether it is harmful especially interesting. The authors show that “bad AI” does not always mean that the technology is inaccurate. There are different possibilities: an AI system may fail and cause harm, or it may work accurately and still cause harm because of how it is used. Facial recognition is a good example. The authors argue that face recognition can work quite accurately under appropriate conditions, but the same capability can also be used for mass surveillance or other harmful purposes. On the other hand, AI cheating detectors are an example where the system itself may not work reliably and can lead to false accusations. To me, the interesting point is that evaluating AI should not stop at asking, “Does the technology work?” We also need to ask, “What is it being used for, what are the consequences, and is AI even the right solution to this problem?” In some cases, the deeper issue may be institutional rather than technical. And this question and topic is trending one to be disucseed in the real world. his also connects to a broader real-world debate about whether the rapid development and deployment of AI should be slowed down because of its potential harms. I think this makes the chapter especially interesting to read, because it encourages us to think not only about what AI is capable of doing, but also about whether, where, and how we should use it.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

The book argues that predictive AI should be treated cautiously unless there is strong evidence that it works. My question is what should count as “strong evidence” in practice, especially in high-stakes settings. Many AI systems may be useful even though they are not perfect, so how should we decide when the benefit is large enough to justify the remaining errors or harms? For example, facial recognition can be useful for tasks such as unlocking phones or identifying people in certain investigations, but the same technology can create serious concerns when used for surveillance. I hope to learn from the discussion how we should balance technical performance, practical usefulness, and potential harm, rather than judging an AI system only by its accuracy.
