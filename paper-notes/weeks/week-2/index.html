What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found it interesting that both papers try to make alignment more explicit rather than having models infer safety behavior only from large amounts of human-labeled examples. Constitutional AI uses a small set of written principles to generate critiques, revisions, and AI preference feedback, greatly reducing the need for human harmlessness labels. What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Constitutional AI relies on a relatively small constitution, while Deliberative Alignment uses much more detailed category-specific specifications. What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

For the Deliberative Alignment paper, since the policy text is removed at inference time, how accurately does the model actually remember it? For the Constitutional AI paper, since a large amount of the harmlessness training data is generated by the model itself, could this introduce systematic bias or distribution shift into the learned behavior? The paper compares AI-supervised models with human-feedback baselines, but it does not seem to study how the model changes as progressively larger fractions of human supervision are replaced by AI-generated supervision.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

It seems like the most alarming part of the Adversarial Reasoning paper is how cheap the attack is. They were hitting target queries in just 240 queries (with an iteration count of 15, plus 1,920 autoregression steps per task) on a single A100, compared to up to 10^4 iterations for traditional token-space attacks. If exploiting test-time compute is this cheap for an attacker, what does that mean for the computational cost of an effective defense? Deliberative Alignment explicitly hides the Chain of thought(CoT) from the judge during RL so the model doesn't learn to generate deceptive reasoning. When a model gets successfully jailbroken, does the internal reasoning trace show it getting tricked step-by-step, or does the CoT just become completely unfaithful to what the model decides to output? Anu Soneye

Sep 8, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback - This work reminds me of Constitutional Classifiers: Defending Against Universal Jailbreaks Across Thousands of Hours of Red Teaming, published in 2025. The central principles introduced in this 2022 paper likely helped inspire that later work. From a formal-methods perspective, Constitutional AI has several compelling parallels. For example, its critique–revision loop resembles an approximate form of counterexample-guided inductive synthesis (CEGIS): the model generates a candidate response, identifies a violation of a constitutional principle, and revises the response accordingly. More broadly, training an LLM to abide by a prescribed set of principles resembles model synthesis, in which a system is constructed to satisfy a given specification. The crucial difference is that neither the critique nor the revised response is formally guaranteed to satisfy the constitution; both are produced and evaluated by fallible language models. Deliberative Alignment: Reasoning Enables Safer Language Models - The approach also requires relatively little direct human supervision because models generate the reasoning traces and responses, while a specification-aware judge model filters the supervised fine-tuning data and supplies the reinforcement-learning signal.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Questions for Both Papers: 1. How does each method resolve conflicts among principles, particularly when those conflicts are subtle, unforeseen, or context-dependent? 2. How scalable are these approaches when the governing principles change over time? Human standards of helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness are unlikely to remain fixed, and different cultures or communities may interpret these values differently. Can the principles be updated or adapted without retraining the entire model?
