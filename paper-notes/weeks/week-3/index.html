Selina Xiao

Sep 10, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

For Discrimination in the Age of Algorithms, I find it interesting that the paper does not treat “the algorithm” as a single object, but separates the trainer from the screener. This makes the source of discrimination more complicated than simply asking whether the final model directly uses a protected attribute, because bias can enter much earlier through the design choices that determine what the model is learning in the first place. I also find it interesting that the authors go further and argue that allowing an algorithm to use protected attributes such as race can sometimes help reduce bias already embedded in other variables. This challenges the intuitive idea that fairness necessarily means removing protected attributes from the model entirely. For Sleeper Agents, I find it interesting that the most concerning result is not simply that safety training sometimes fails to remove a backdoor, but that adversarial training can make the problem less visible. I also find it particularly interesting that backdoors associated with chain-of-thought reasoning about deception were more persistent, and that this increased persistence remained even after the chain-of-thought itself was distilled away. That suggests the effect is not simply caused by the model explicitly generating deceptive reasoning at inference time.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

For Discrimination in the Age of Algorithms, my question is how we should decide which part of an algorithmic decision process should actually be held responsible for an observed disparity. If the disparity can come from the objective being optimized, the available features, the training data, the learned screening rule, or structural inequalities outside the model, it seems difficult to determine where discrimination “belongs” and what the appropriate intervention should be. For Sleeper Agents, my main question is how much these results tell us about deceptive behavior that might actually emerge naturally in a model. The authors deliberately construct these deceptive or backdoored models as “model organisms of misalignment,” and they explicitly state that they have not observed deceptive instrumental alignment arising naturally in current systems. This makes the experiment useful for testing whether existing safety methods could remove such behavior, but it also makes me wonder whether the persistence they observe is partly a consequence of how strongly and deliberately the backdoor was implanted.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

The Sleeper Agents paper is honestly wild. It was really intriguing to find out that standard safety training like SFT or RLHF failed to remove the backdoor, and adversarial training, the authors determine, can actually teach the model to hide it better. Instead of unlearning the deceptive behavior and dropping it, the model just gets better at recognizing its triggers so it avoids being penalized during training. It completely breaks the assumption that a model is genuinely safe just because it behaves well during fine-tuning. Discrimination in the age of Algorithms: It was interesting to find out that bias enters through the input variables, the training sample, and crucially, the choice of outcome measure, and was an eye-opener for me. And the fact that algorithms make discrimination easier to detect than human decisions and race "aware" models beat race "blind" ones was interesting too.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

For Sleeper Agents, how do we actually catch this phenomenon in practice? If the model knows it's being evaluated, catching it I feel is basically impossible because the model just won't act maliciously on purpose to cover its tracks. I'd love to dig into the systems engineering side of defending against this. Are labs trying to detect these hidden backdoors at the training infrastructure level, or is the defense purely focused on better post-training red-teaming? For the Kleinberg paper, The paper says that race "aware" models dominate race "blind" ones but I think the law generally prohibits using race, is the fix like legal reform or is there a technical route to the same gain without the protected variable? and When a biased proxy variable is deeply seeded in a company's data infrastructure, what is the practical engineering path to swapping it out for a truer measure without breaking downstream accuracy?

Alan Liang

Sep 11, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Paper 1: I found it interesting that adversarial training could reduce failures on the red-team prompts without removing the planted backdoor. The model became better at distinguishing those prompts from the original trigger, so improved test performance could give a misleading impression of safety. I also found it interesting that backdoors trained using deceptive chain-of-thought reasoning could remain persistent after distillation, when the model was trained to produce answers without the written reasoning. Paper 2: I found it interesting that allowing a model to use gender could sometimes help it correct the effects of biased manager ratings. Without that information, the model might treat the same rating as equally informative for everyone, even when the ratings were produced differently for men and women. I also found it interesting that accurately predicting past hiring decisions could reproduce discrimination. Better prediction would not necessarily solve the problem if the model was learning to imitate biased decisions.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Paper 1: Why were larger models better at retaining their backdoors during reinforcement-learning safety training? Why was supervised fine-tuning generally more effective than reinforcement learning at weakening the backdoors, and when did that advantage disappear? What would a convincing test of backdoor removal look like if we did not already know the trigger? I hope to learn how defenses that specifically target a backdoor differ from general safety training. I also want to understand what distillation preserves that allows the behavior to remain without the written reasoning. Paper 2: When does including a protected characteristic help correct bias, and when might it reinforce discrimination? If the outcome labels are also biased, how can we tell whether the model’s predictions have actually improved? How much representative data would we need to learn that a rating has different meanings across groups? I want to see a simple numerical example comparing a model that uses gender with one that does not. I also hope to understand why correcting a biased input is different from correcting the outcome the model is trained to predict.
