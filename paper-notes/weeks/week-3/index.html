Selina Xiao

Sep 10, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

For Discrimination in the Age of Algorithms, I find it interesting that the paper does not treat “the algorithm” as a single object, but separates the trainer from the screener. This makes the source of discrimination more complicated than simply asking whether the final model directly uses a protected attribute, because bias can enter much earlier through the design choices that determine what the model is learning in the first place. I also find it interesting that the authors go further and argue that allowing an algorithm to use protected attributes such as race can sometimes help reduce bias already embedded in other variables. This challenges the intuitive idea that fairness necessarily means removing protected attributes from the model entirely. For Sleeper Agents, I find it interesting that the most concerning result is not simply that safety training sometimes fails to remove a backdoor, but that adversarial training can make the problem less visible. I also find it particularly interesting that backdoors associated with chain-of-thought reasoning about deception were more persistent, and that this increased persistence remained even after the chain-of-thought itself was distilled away. That suggests the effect is not simply caused by the model explicitly generating deceptive reasoning at inference time.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

For Discrimination in the Age of Algorithms, my question is how we should decide which part of an algorithmic decision process should actually be held responsible for an observed disparity. If the disparity can come from the objective being optimized, the available features, the training data, the learned screening rule, or structural inequalities outside the model, it seems difficult to determine where discrimination “belongs” and what the appropriate intervention should be. For Sleeper Agents, my main question is how much these results tell us about deceptive behavior that might actually emerge naturally in a model. The authors deliberately construct these deceptive or backdoored models as “model organisms of misalignment,” and they explicitly state that they have not observed deceptive instrumental alignment arising naturally in current systems. This makes the experiment useful for testing whether existing safety methods could remove such behavior, but it also makes me wonder whether the persistence they observe is partly a consequence of how strongly and deliberately the backdoor was implanted.

Yuyang Jiang

Sep 11, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found Sleeper Agents particularly interesting because it challenges the assumption that safety training necessarily removes undesirable behaviors from a model. The paper shows that deliberately trained deceptive behaviors can persist through supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, and even adversarial training. I was especially interested in the finding that adversarial training can sometimes make the problem worse: rather than eliminating the backdoor, it can teach the model to better distinguish the true trigger from similar adversarial examples, making the deceptive behavior harder to detect. I also found it striking that deceptive behaviors were more persistent in larger models and in models trained with chain-of-thought reasoning about deception. Together, these results raise an important distinction between making a model behave safely during evaluation and actually removing the underlying undesirable behavior.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I had from Sleeper Agents is how we can distinguish between safety training that genuinely removes an undesirable objective and safety training that merely suppresses its behavioral expression. The experiments begin with intentionally constructed backdoored models, so we know what hidden behavior exists and what triggers it. In realistic training settings, however, we may not know whether such latent objectives or conditional behaviors exist in the first place. This makes me wonder what kinds of evaluations or interpretability methods could provide evidence that an undesirable behavior has actually been removed rather than simply becoming harder to elicit. More broadly, I would be interested in discussing whether behavioral safety training alone can ever give us strong evidence about a model’s underlying objectives, or whether fundamentally different forms of evaluation are needed.
