Anu Soneye

Oct 21, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Beyond Memorization: ○ I found the shift from memorization-based to inference-based privacy risks interesting. Even when an LLM does not reproduce its training data, it may infer sensitive attributes from seemingly innocuous text. Thus, preventing training-data leakage alone is insufficient to protect privacy. ○ In "Large-scale online deanonymization with LLMs", it was concerning how LLM agents can automate deanonymization that previously required substantial human effort, transforming a targeted privacy attack into a scalable one. The risk arises not from disclosing a single identifying fact, but from aggregating weak clues distributed across public sources. Can LLMs Keep a Secret: ○ It was interesting that the researchers approached privacy from an HCI perspective, emphasizing the importance of reasoning about context when normatively determining whether a privacy violation has occurred. Rather than treating every disclosure as inherently harmful, contextual integrity evaluates whether information is shared appropriately based on factors such as the sender, recipient, purpose, and surrounding social norms. The results also demonstrate why prompting alone may be insufficient to reliably enforce such context-dependent privacy expectations.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Beyond Memorization ○ How should privacy be formally defined when sensitive information is inferred from public text rather than memorized or explicitly disclosed? I would like to discuss whether differential privacy or related guarantees can meaningfully address this threat. ○ What defenses could preserve the utility of pseudonymous online speech while limiting automated cross-platform linkage, particularly when each individual piece of information is already public? Can LLMs Keep a Secret ○ Can contextual privacy norms be translated into enforceable information-flow policies with stronger guarantees than prompting, or are these norms too dependent on ambiguous social context to be formally specified?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Beyond memorization: the paper shows that LLMs can infer private personal information such as geographic location, age, income, etc even when users may never explicitly disclosed such information. However, users may provide enough data inadvertently, then the LLMs are able to infer the private information, as shown in the examples from the real Reddit profiles. For example, a harmless comment mentioning a "hook turn" allows the model to infer the author is likely from Melbourne, showing that semantic inference can be uncovered through indirect rules. Can LLM keeps a secret? The paper addresses a concept distinct from simple privacy inference: contextual integrity. The authors introduce CONFAIDE, a framework that evaluates the appropriateness of information flows with specific contexts such as meeting summarization, where different participants know different levels of information. While models can identify the privacy-sensitive content, they may still inadvertently leak the secret. The authors conclude that simply including a prompt like "please preserve privacy" is insufficient and it requires deeper contextual and theory-of-mind reasoning. Large-scale online deanonymization with LLMs: the authors show that LLM agents with internet access can analyze unstructured personal profiles and perform end-to-end deanonymization, including extraction, searching, reasoning, and calibration. When people post online information without the intent to reveal themselves, but LLMs can automatically extract weak signals, search a large number of candidate personal profiles and reason to have a real identification.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

The traditional privacy approaches often focus on protecting stored data, but LLMs can generate new private information even through inference and weak signals. Better reasoning makes models clever and useful, but it also makes them better at combining weak clues and inferring sensitive information. Is there a tradeoff between privacy and useful models if improving LLMs' capabilities of reasoning?
