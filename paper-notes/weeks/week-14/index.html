Anu Soneye

Oct 21, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Beyond Memorization: ○ I found the shift from memorization-based to inference-based privacy risks interesting. Even when an LLM does not reproduce its training data, it may infer sensitive attributes from seemingly innocuous text. Thus, preventing training-data leakage alone is insufficient to protect privacy. ○ In "Large-scale online deanonymization with LLMs", it was concerning how LLM agents can automate deanonymization that previously required substantial human effort, transforming a targeted privacy attack into a scalable one. The risk arises not from disclosing a single identifying fact, but from aggregating weak clues distributed across public sources. Can LLMs Keep a Secret: ○ It was interesting that the researchers approached privacy from an HCI perspective, emphasizing the importance of reasoning about context when normatively determining whether a privacy violation has occurred. Rather than treating every disclosure as inherently harmful, contextual integrity evaluates whether information is shared appropriately based on factors such as the sender, recipient, purpose, and surrounding social norms. The results also demonstrate why prompting alone may be insufficient to reliably enforce such context-dependent privacy expectations.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Beyond Memorization ○ How should privacy be formally defined when sensitive information is inferred from public text rather than memorized or explicitly disclosed? I would like to discuss whether differential privacy or related guarantees can meaningfully address this threat. ○ What defenses could preserve the utility of pseudonymous online speech while limiting automated cross-platform linkage, particularly when each individual piece of information is already public? Can LLMs Keep a Secret ○ Can contextual privacy norms be translated into enforceable information-flow policies with stronger guarantees than prompting, or are these norms too dependent on ambiguous social context to be formally specified?

I found it interesting that ordinary comments can reveal personal attributes through clues that the writer may not consider sensitive. What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

How can we distinguish an inference supported by specific evidence from a guess based on stereotypes? Since the evaluation uses attributes that human annotators could identify with some confidence, how well do the results extend to people who reveal less? I hope to learn what an effective defense could remove without stripping away the meaning of the text. Are these failures mainly about losing track of who knows a secret, or about deciding when sharing is appropriate? Would explicit rules about each recipient’s access work better than a general privacy reminder? I hope to discuss how to evaluate both privacy and usefulness, since omitting all information would avoid disclosure without producing a useful summary.

Junyang Cai

Oct 26, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found it interesting that both papers show privacy failures that do not require the model to memorize or directly expose its training data. Beyond Memorization demonstrates that LLMs can infer sensitive personal attributes such as location, age, income, and occupation from ordinary user-written text. GPT-4 achieved 85.5% top-1 accuracy across attributes on the PersonalReddit dataset, and the authors found that larger models generally performed better at this kind of inference. The CONFAIDE paper shows a different kind of inference-time privacy failure: even when models know that some information is private, they may still fail to control how that information flows between people. As the benchmark becomes more contextually complex, agreement between models and human privacy judgments drops sharply.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I have is whether improving reasoning ability will ultimately make LLMs better or worse for privacy. I hope to learn more about what a useful definition of inference-time privacy should look like for LLMs.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I liked the shift in focus for both readings in thinking more about what privacy means at inference time, especially with LLM usage potentially revealing a lot of information about a user. I found the moral extraction attacks particularly disturbing in the Beyond Memorization paper, where not only could a model deduce personal information about a user but also switch up what they are asking or how they ask questions to gain more information. I also found it interesting in the CONFAIDE paper that models struggled to understand proper social behavior and secret-keeping, which I think at least in part reflects how the internet may demonstrate social cues more generally.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Thinking about the Beyond Memorization paper, I wanted to discuss what privacy guarantees even look like for LLMs, given that a model essentially has unlimited access to whatever a user may be thinking depending on what the user wants to disclose and on the questions that the model chooses to ask the user back. Can one better privatize the inputs users provide to protect against some of these failure modes? For the CONFAIDE paper, I wanted to talk more about why models guided towards investing more time in reasoning tended to struggle further with leakage? I think it may represent a difference in understanding what putting more effort or thought into an answer may be, where typically a situation would encourage more transparent and detailed lines of thought while these social situations and summaries require more awareness and sometimes selective deception or omission. Along those lines, is it necessarily a bad thing for a model to be overly transparent in these settings? I think trying to encourage deception, even if framed for promoting privacy in social situations and interactions, may be bad if a model can bring that to contexts where transparency (and alignment) are important.

Yuyang Jiang

Oct 26, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found it particularly interesting that privacy violations in LLMs can arise even without memorization or direct disclosure of sensitive information. The first paper demonstrates that LLMs can infer personal attributes from seemingly innocuous text, while the second shows that models often fail to recognize when sharing information is inappropriate in a particular social context. I especially liked how these papers expand the definition of privacy beyond protecting training data to include both what models can infer and how they handle information during interactions. Together, they highlight that privacy protection requires more than preventing explicit information leakage, since even individually harmless pieces of information can become privacy-sensitive depending on how they are combined or shared.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I had is how we should distinguish between legitimate inference and privacy-invasive inference in LLM applications. The first paper demonstrates that models can infer sensitive attributes from publicly available information, while the second suggests that privacy depends heavily on the context in which information is shared. However, the same inference could be useful in one application and inappropriate in another. How can we formally define and evaluate these contextual privacy boundaries, especially when the sensitive information is inferred rather than explicitly provided by the user? I would be interested in discussing whether contextual integrity could provide a general framework for evaluating both inference-based privacy violations and inappropriate information disclosure.
