Selina Xiao

Sep 29, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

The agent UQ paper argues that uncertainty should not just be a confidence score attached to the final answer. In an interactive agent, uncertainty evolves over a trajectory of actions, observations, and environment states, and an action such as asking a clarification question can actively reduce uncertainty rather than simply propagate it forward. This makes uncertainty feel much closer to a state variable for sequential decision making than a passive evaluation metric. The difference-awareness paper makes a surprisingly similar point about fairness. Treating invariance across demographic groups as the objective can hide cases where different treatment is factually or normatively appropriate, so the authors separate difference awareness from contextual awareness. I especially liked their result that explicitly prompting models to be less biased can actually reduce difference awareness, while prompting for more difference awareness can improve DiffAware but hurt CtxtAware. This looks almost like a precision-recall tradeoff and shows that a single scalar notion of "less bias" is probably too coarse.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

For the agent UQ paper, one question I had is whether uncertainty should ultimately be optimized jointly with the agent policy rather than estimated after the fact. If an agent can choose actions specifically to reduce uncertainty, then the relevant object may be something like the expected value of information of an action, and I wonder how this could be separated from ordinary task reward or incorporated into an RL or control objective. I am also curious how one can distinguish genuinely problematic epistemic uncertainty from cases where there are simply several equally valid next actions, especially since the paper itself points out that agent trajectories contain heterogeneous uncertainty from the agent, user, and tools. For the difference-awareness paper, my main question is how the desired notion of contextual awareness should be specified without simply replacing one fixed fairness definition with another set of values chosen by benchmark designers. The descriptive tasks have relatively clear answers, but normative tasks necessarily encode judgments about when differential treatment is justified. It would be interesting to formulate DiffAware and CtxtAware as a constrained or multiobjective optimization problem and ask whether there is a meaningful Pareto frontier, rather than searching for one prompt or alignment method that maximizes both.

Nana Xia

Sep 29, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

In the paper “Uncertainty Quantification in LLM Agents,” I found that uncertainty is not only something we measure at the end of a decision process. In traditional LLM uncertainty work, we often focus on how confident the model is about its final answer. But for an interactive agent, uncertainty can actually affect what the agent should do next. At the same time, uncertainty from earlier steps can also propagate through the trajectory, so the paper argues that we need to model both uncertainty propagation and uncertainty reduction through interaction. In “Fairness through Difference Awareness,” I found it interesting that a simple debiasing approach does not necessarily make a model fairer. The paper argues that fairness should not only be evaluated by asking whether the model treats different groups the same. Instead, we should ask whether the model recognizes group differences when those differences are relevant, and avoids using them when they are not. Simply encouraging the model to pay more attention to group differences is not a complete solution either, because that can reduce Contextual Awareness and make the model differentiate when it should not. To me, this means fairness cannot be handled with a one-size-fits-all “debias everything” rule. The harder problem is deciding when group differences should matter and when they should not.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I have from “Fairness through Difference Awareness” is how this idea should actually be implemented in a real model. If fairness sometimes requires recognizing meaningful group differences, should those differences be explicitly represented in the model, or should they mainly be handled through domain-specific instructions, data, or fine-tuning? The paper shows that a general fairness instruction can sometimes make the model ignore legitimate differences, while encouraging difference awareness too broadly can also create problems. This makes me wonder whether we should move away from a single generalized notion of fairness and instead develop domain-specific fairness criteria.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Wang et al. talks about the relevance of group differences, and the cases where ignoring the differences is actually detrimental to the result. Pursuing fairness itself should not be a consistent thing. I think it is interesting that one of the topics most heavily pursued in ML has cases where models perform worse when they are fair. It makes me wonder what other ML topics should be worked backwards to move ahead. They also never build a method to check improve the model's context to decide whether or not it must be fair. They only build benchmarks to test them. Oh et al. discuss the stages of uncertainty (changes in the confidence scoring) within the reasoning process of a model. It is interesting that a model with clarity can fall into confusion and recover within the same reasoning process and end the prompt with a high degree of confidence. It may also be important to note these changes (or report them) when making a decision on how the results of these prompts are to be used. It is also important because making a bad decision (which had low confidence) in the intermediate stages of the trajectory may lead to worse results in the long term, and lots of wasted effort.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

As I was discussing earlier, Wang et al. never create a system to aid the models' contextual decision making ability, and only provide a benchmark. How can we achieve a state for models such that they are able to distinguish the degree of fairness to be used from the context itself? Could it be based on alignment or post training methods? With newer models (and agentic models), we are able to steer mid-prompt and models can pause to question the user too. Oh et al. talk about uncertainty in the middle of the trajectory, why cant we stop the thinking when the model is uncertain and ask the user? I guess the question moves to the degree to which the model's confidence must fall before requesting user interference. I am curious to know how often models lose confidence and which tasks appear the most when confidence is lost. It might be the case that coding tasks are high confidence and image generation is low, for example. If we are able to simply alleviate one half of the problem there might no longer be an issue to begin with.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Fairness through Difference Awareness: The authors propose new metrics and a benchmark for context aware fairness. They show that SOTA models excel at current fairness benchmarks but their metrics introduce difference awareness and context awareness, which models fail at. This is a much more interesting and nuanced point of view of fairness than prescribing that models should be fair to all groups in all scenarios, and it captures cases like the paper's example of Gemini generating racially diverse images of Nazis. Uncertainty Quantification: This paper collects benchmarks on uncertainty estimation in agents and proposes that more work needs to be done to collect turn-level annotations of agent trajectories.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Fairness through Difference Awareness: An interesting finding was that instructing models to be more fair actually decreases performance, except on the asylum related questions. It's not clear why this is the case, and why asylum related questions are the exception to this pattern. I'm curious if a simple approach of learning which of two prompts to use (depending on the context, pick a prompt for DiffAware or CtxtAware) would improve performance. Uncertainty Quantification: There is prior work on when LLMs should ask clarification questions in multi-turn conversation with humans. I know the paper differentiates itself from this task setup because of the addition of the environment, but it would be interesting to see if any approaches from multi-turn conversation could apply in this case as well.
