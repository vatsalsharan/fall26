What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

This relaxed definition of differential privacy seems to quantify this notion of difference across input data distributions in a different way, where one focuses on the cumulative distance across distributions after some number of timesteps rather than the worst case distance one could have at a given timestep. This measurement is interesting in that it tries to capture how much possible leakage there can be across two processes more generally as a function of their inputs and how much of the distribution is really important to someone to cover for the guarantee.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One of the main advantages of this process appears to be in making the math nice in allowing for a summation across moments of the distribution to quantify total privacy guarantees, but I wonder how does this connect to the particular aspects of a given function's data leakage? Given DP is more of a worst-case scenario, and this relaxation allows for a tighter bound, can we get even tighter somehow incorporating a function's particular ability to protect data? Perhaps analyzing a system’s properties for its function (ie. gradient/curvature/smoothness) generally combined with measurable properties of a dataset (ie. Sparsity, smoothness, clusters/attribute-specific correlation, etc.)?

What I found interesting is the intuition behind the definition. Standard differential privacy only looks at the worst case, the single output that gives the most away. The Gaussian mechanism has an unbounded worst case, so standard differential privacy has no value for it at all, while the Renyi version gives it a short exact formula. Converting back to the usual two-parameter form takes one line, and you only do it once at the end.

My question is what happens when we want to compare two mechanisms using their curves instead of a single number. The Laplace curve levels off at a ceiling while the Gaussian one keeps growing, so depending on the parameters the two can cross. Then which mechanism leaks less depends on which part of the curve you care about. I’d like to understand how you would choose between them in that situation.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found it interesting that "Renyi Differential Privacy (RDP)" paper mentioned that the pure DP could be too restrictive for some Gaussian mechanisms. A single Gaussian mechanism may satisfy many possible parameter pairs (ε1, δ1), (ε2, δ2),.... and does not correspond to just one pair (ε, δ). However, choosing only one pair would throw away lots of useful information about the full privacy behavior of the mechanism. This becomes even more difficult when many privacy-preserving mechanisms are composed repeatedly. Instead, RDP addresses the problem by keeping a richer description of the privacy loss through the parameter α, and it preserved more information about the privacy-loss distribution and made repeated Gaussian mechanisms much easier to analyze.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

RDP seems very useful for keeping track of privacy loss, especially when the same private data are accessed through many repeated mechanisms. However, if RDP preserves more information and makes composition easier, why do we usually convert to the final RDP guarantee back to (ε, δ)-DP?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

One thing I found interesting is that point is that delta silently merges two completely different failure modes. Under one, privacy degrades gracefully. Under the other, with probability delta the secret is simply exposed. A single epsilon:delta pair cannot tell you which mechanism you have. The standard advice to set delta ≪ 1/N doesn’t resolve this, rather it makes the bad case rare. Another point that is interesting is that for a fixed alpha the Renyi Differential Privacy parameters can simply be added. This involves some linearity of moment generating functions which I find to be very interesting (and cool for lack of a better term)

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Thirteen values worked empirically for their mixture of randomized-response, Laplace, and Gaussian mechanisms. But the grid is a heuristic, and the optimum for an adversarially chosen sequence of mechanisms could fall between points. Is there a guarantee on grid-induced looseness, or is this a case where practice outran theory and nobody minded because it worked? Another point is that we see that the result trades alpha down by 2^c while multiplying epsilon by 3^c. Correlated records i.e. families, households, social graphs, repeated entries for one person are the common real case, not the exotic one. Is that degradation intrinsic to the Rényi framing, or an artifact of a proof that goes by induction from c=1?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I find it interesting that Rényi differential privacy provides a more flexible way to measure privacy loss, especially when multiple mechanisms are composed. In particular, I like how the paper uses Rényi divergence to characterize the difference between the output distributions of a mechanism on two neighboring datasets, rather than relying only on the standard \((\varepsilon,\delta)\)-DP formulation. Another interesting point is that the privacy parameters can be added under composition for a fixed Rényi order, which makes it easier to track privacy loss over multiple steps. I also find the connection to membership inference interesting. Since Rényi differential privacy limits how distinguishable the outputs are when one data point is added or removed, it seems closely related to how confidently we can determine whether a particular example was included in a model's training set.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

I'm curious about how Rényi differential privacy affects the effectiveness of membership inference attacks. If a model is trained with strong Rényi differential privacy guarantees, can we derive a theoretical bound on how accurately an attacker can distinguish between training members and non-members? I'm particularly interested in whether the Rényi divergence bound can be translated into a bound on the true positive rate and false positive rate of a membership inference test. I'm also wondering how this extends from individual data points to an entire dataset. For example, even if strong privacy guarantees make it difficult to identify whether a single example was used in training, could an auditor still detect that a disclosed training dataset contains a significant fraction of examples that were never used? I'd like to better understand how privacy guarantees relate to the statistical power of membership inference, and whether there are fundamental limits to verifying training-data provenance.
