Anu Soneye

Oct 22, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Position: Considerations for Differentially Private Learning with Large-Scale Public Pretraining ○ I found it interesting that DP-FTRL provides a formal privacy guarantee without assuming that clients are sampled uniformly at random. Uniform sampling is difficult to enforce in a deployed system because device availability depends on conditions such as whether the device is charging and connected to an unmetered network. This demonstrates how the validity of a formal guarantee can depend on whether its assumptions correspond to the behavior of the underlying system. ○ I also found the relationship between computational efficiency and privacy interesting. Public pretraining allowed the Gboard models to reach the target utility in fewer federated-learning rounds, thereby reducing the repeated participation of users and producing a stronger privacy guarantee. Federated Learning of Gboard Language Models with Differential Privacy ○ Unlike many differentially private learning algorithms, DP-FTRL does not require clients to be sampled uniformly at random. In a deployed system such as Gboard, device participation depends on practical conditions, including whether the device is charging or connected to an unmetered network. This makes the paper a useful example of adapting a formal privacy guarantee to the constraints of a real system. Particularly interesting was the relationship between computational efficiency and privacy. Public pretraining allowed the models to reach their target utility in fewer rounds. By reducing repeated user participation, this approach strengthened the privacy guarantee rather than merely lowering the computational cost.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Position: Considerations for Differentially Private Learning with Large-Scale Public Pretraining ○ One counterargument to the paper’s central position is that the term privacy-preserving learning could simply be refined for the transfer-learning paradigm. Researchers could specify that differential privacy protects only the fine-tuning data, rather than implying that the entire model is private. This seems more practical than requiring a completely privacy-friendly pretrained model, which may be difficult to construct at the scale necessary for effective transfer learning. ○ I agree that LLMs can amplify existing privacy violations by making sensitive information on the internet easier to retrieve and disseminate. However, this does not invalidate the privacy guarantee provided to individuals in the fine-tuning dataset. Instead, it suggests that pretraining-data privacy and fine-tuning-data privacy should be treated as separate properties that require different protections. Federated Learning of Gboard Language Models with Differential Privacy ○ One question I had was how the system verifies constraints such as the maximum number of client participations and the minimum separation between participations. If an implementation error violates these assumptions, how much does the claimed privacy guarantee weaken? Reading the two papers together also raised a question for me. While the Gboard paper uses public pretraining to strengthen its formal privacy guarantees, the position paper questions whether web-scraped pretraining data should be considered non-sensitive in the first place. How should a deployed system communicate a guarantee that protects Gboard users’ fine-tuning data but not the individuals represented in its public pretraining data?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Considerations for DP learning: I know this paper is focused on the privacy aspects of ethical issues of using "public" data online, but what struck me about it was actually the fact that even if a user consents to posting their information publicly online, that doesn't automatically mean they also consent to the data being used in any particular way. I'm disappointed (but not surprised) that publicly available data seems to be treated like it's automatically okay to use for training. I also found it concerning that describing a model as differentially private could give people the impression that all of its training data is protected, even though that protection may only apply to fine-tuning. And even when DP does apply, I still feel like the consent question remains, since protecting someone’s privacy doesn’t automatically mean they agreed to that use of their data. Federated learning: I found the strategies they used for protections (limiting each phone's influence, adding randomness, limiting repeated participation) really interesting! It kind of reminded me of Apple's "Learning with Privacy at Scale": https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/learning-with-privacy-at-scale where they used private reports to learn which emojis were popular across users without needing to know each person's emoji history, since in both papers they're learning useful patterns across many users while limiting what can be learned about any one person.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Considerations for DP learning: I'm curious how we can find ways to better inform users (since majority of people posting stuff online are not going to be AI/ML experts who even know what "differentially private" means). Is there a way to explain to them what's going on in a way that they can actually follow so that we can get informed consent (including different levels of it, like allowing their data to be used for training with or without differential privacy protections, or not allowing to to be used for training at all)? Federated learning: This paper actually also kind of reminded me of the Performative Prediction paper from a while ago, and it got me wondering: even if a keyboard learns from people in a privacy preserving way, is it actually learning their existing preferences, or partly learning preferences that its own suggestions helped create? Like how do its suggestions actually influence users' behaviors and how does that behavior then influence future predictions?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

In the Gboard federated learning paper, I thought it was interesting how guarantees over the entire system can exist by clipping and adding noise locally without taking a uniform random sample across the entire population. It really speaks to how different advancements can come from the real-world constraints of a given problem. I also really liked the pretraining paper, as it raises an excellent point about potential shortcuts and leaks arising from pretraining on public data, where essentially one could leverage the accuracy gain of a non-private dataset and overpromise performance and still sell a model as private because of the finetuning process.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

For the GBoard paper, I wonder how much in practice these models suffer from the privacy-accuracy tradeoff across smaller subsets of the population despite working at such a large scale preventing overall accuracy damage. Does adding noise on the local level introduce any fairness concerns in the type of accuracy lost, where certain specific quirks of unique dialects could be represented privately in theory but aren’t in practice because noise is spread everywhere? Also, does the pure hardware bottleneck in needing to train locally as part of the federated process limit information gain in a significant way (compared to a hypothetical approach where every user had local access to big hardware)? I’d be curious to know more specifically about the type of information hypothetically lost to hardware limitations and if there is any trend to the type of content lost, especially as it relates to the long tail concerns brought up in the large-scale pretraining paper. Specific to this work, I wonder how much overlap there really is between the benchmark evaluation sets and the model pretraining corpus they test on. Does performance hold up on guaranteed unseen data, like a proprietary set that some researcher may have access to?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found the contrast between the two papers especially interesting. The Gboard paper shows that public pretraining can make differentially private learning much more practical: pretraining on public C4 data reduces the number of federated training rounds needed to reach a target utility, which in turn improves the privacy guarantee. What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I have is where we should draw the boundary between “public” and “private” data when evaluating a differentially private system. I hope to learn more about how we should evaluate privacy at the full system level rather than only through the formal DP guarantee of one training stage.

Aman Desai

Oct 29, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Position: Considerations for Differentially Private Learning with Large-Scale Public Pretraining: I really liked this paper's discussion of how people may feel that their privacy has been violated by a model being able to output public data about them. In general, I have been thinking about privacy has being a relatively strict distinction in that data is either private or public. However, as the paper discusses, someone's information may be publicly available without their consent or intent. It seems like this could very naturally occur even for someone who never shares something online (for example, they may be in the background of someone else's publicly available photo). In general, it seems very difficult to precisely make the distinction between public and private when training models on web-scraped data. Federated Learning of Gboard Language Models with Differential Privacy: I had not realized that Differential Privacy and Federated Learning were so aligned in goal, but different in execution. More specifically, I had assumed that any algorithm for one would automatically satisfy the other, though this is not exactly the case. At the same time, the hypothesis the paper considers that the "utility is approximately non-decreasing if the noise multiplier and clients per round (lower bounded by report goal) proportionally increase" does make sense for both DP and FL. Broadly speaking, higher noise multipliers should decrease performance while more clients per round increases performance, so there should be a way to increase these quantities together without sacrificing too much performance.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Position: Considerations for Differentially Private Learning with Large-Scale Public Pretraining: Would the paper's concerns be addressed if we trained a secondary model on synthetic data produced by a DP model or inference procedure? This seems like a way to guarantee privacy without decreasing performance or making any assumptions about what in the original dataset is private. Federated Learning of Gboard Language Models with Differential Privacy: To what extent does the FL training help with DP (and vice versa)?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Thought it was interesting how the GBoard paper solves practicality for having privacy guarantees for GBoard language models. DP-FedAvg fails due to there needing to be uniformity in selection for device selection. Real systems fail due to how phones only join training when it is both charging and on Wifi. Privacy ends up being an engineering problem that adapts the theory to reality, where timers and estimates on population sizes maintain the epsilon privacy (even epsilon is quite large). Position paper gets a 96.2% accuracy on cifar without touching the cifar data. Interesting because if a model doesn't see the data and does nearly as well, then it may rather be a domain adaptation problem rather than learning something new privately - is this a problem is a separate question. But these pretrained models are too big to run on phones so sending private data to third party for further processing may undo the original point of private training.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Wonder what level of epsilon actually constitutes meaningful rather than just something is better than nothing. What does good benchmarking also look like for something like privacy? Just tailbound analysis ? What does practicality mean here?

Just from the introduction, they give a new way to define privacy using contextual integrity and split it into tiers: is the information itself sensitive? Is this flow appropriate in this context? Then multiple flows and multiple parties, which requires reasoning and theory of mind. I find the tiered framing interesting. However, the experiments feel mostly like collecting and measuring data, and I wish they discussed more about how we should build models that handle this.

[paper1] How did they look into this problem and quantify both the issue itself and the LLM's ability to do it? Did they only try some prompts, or did they build a whole new benchmark? What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Position: Considerations for Differentially Private Learning with Large-Scale Public Pretraining: It's interesting to find out that calling a model "privacy-preserving" just because fine-tuning uses DP is misleading when its "public" web-scraped pre-training data still leaks memorized personal info like Peter W.'s phone number in GPT-2. Also that pre-training on ImageNet and privately fine-tuning on CIFAR-10 mostly measures transfer learning across overlapping classes rather than real private learning. Federated Learning of Gboard Language Models with Differential Privacy: Using DP-FTRL let Google get formal DP guarantees in production without uniform client sampling, which is impossible when phones only train while charging on Wi-Fi was really intriguing to me. Reading these two papers together is also funny because Gboard relies on public C4 pre-training to cut ~1,000 training rounds and hit its DP targets, which is the exact setup the first paper critiques.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Position: Considerations for Differentially Private Learning with Large-Scale Public Pretraining: High DP utility usually requires huge pre-trained models that are too big to run on phones, forcing users to upload sensitive data to cloud servers. When is formal DP on a cloud model actually a better privacy trade-off than a non-DP model that keeps raw data entirely on the device? Federated Learning of Gboard Language Models with Differential Privacy: The authors note in the appendix that their formal DP guarantees only cover a single training run and leave out hyperparameter tuning and A/B testing model selection. How much privacy is actually lost in practice from those uncounted tuning runs, and why don't production teams fold tuning into the total privacy budget?
