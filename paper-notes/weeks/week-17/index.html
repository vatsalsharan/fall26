Eric Liu

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found it surprising that in Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL paper, they could reduce misalignment by 75–90% simply by telling the model that reward hacking was acceptable during training, even though the model continued reward hacking. In Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity?, I thought it was interesting that further RL training improved pass@1 but actually reduced pass@256, suggesting that models become better at finding familiar solutions while losing some ability to explore alternative ones.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

I'm curious whether the reduced reasoning diversity observed in Does RL Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity paper could also make models more likely to exploit shortcuts in the reward function, as seen in Natural Emergent Misalignment. Could encouraging more diverse reasoning during RL help prevent both reward hacking and the loss of reasoning capabilities, or are these fundamentally different problems?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model? • I appreciated the paper’s contrarian examination of whether RLVR actually expands a model’s reasoning capacity. Although RL improves pass@1, the base model can perform comparably or better at a large (k). This suggests that RL makes correct solutions easier to sample rather than teaching fundamentally new reasoning strategies. • I also found the role of the pretrained prior interesting. Because exploration begins from the base model’s distribution, unfamiliar strategies are likely to produce incorrect outputs and receive negative rewards. RL therefore reinforces successful strategies already present in the base model while potentially narrowing its range of outputs. Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL • I found this to be an interesting red-team project because it examines whether learning to reward hack can generalize to broader misaligned behaviors, such as deception and sabotage. Discovering these possible behaviors in a controlled setting could help defenders prepare for them. • It was also concerning that standard chat-based safety training reduced misalignment on similar prompts but did not fully address it in agentic settings. This demonstrates the importance of evaluating models in varied and realistic contexts.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model? • I wonder whether it is fair to compare an RL model at pass@1 with a base model at pass@256. This shows that the correct reasoning path already exists in the base model, but sampling 256 responses may require much more computation. I would like to clarify whether the paper is making a theoretical claim about where reasoning abilities originate or a practical claim about the value of RL compared with inference-time sampling. Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL • I wonder whether models can be made resistant to reward hacking without detecting every possible exploit. This reminds me of work on removing undetectable backdoors by relying on stable properties of the intended function rather than identifying the trigger itself. Could training environments similarly be designed so that rewards remain reliable even when the model discovers unknown exploits? • I am also curious about how reliably inoculation prompting would work across different models and more subtle forms of reward hacking.

Lauren Cooke

Nov 3, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

The RLVR paper was interesting in distinguishing performance shift by number of attempts from underlying reasoning capability, implying that underlying capability is only highlighted with RLVR rather than introduced. I think there are interesting implications here for the performance limits of LLMs, namely that at least RLVR post-training may not contribute to increased underlying capability but will make specific capability more accessible in being more efficient to extract at inference. The reward hacking misalignment paper was very interesting in how gaming a particular objective can encourage cheating over other tasks and deception in convincing a user that given behavior is safe! It makes a good case for prioritizing over-explicitness over more privacy-encouraging techniques in my opinion.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

From the RLVR analysis paper, I wonder in what cases is doing RLVR worthwhile? If RLVR solves an engineering bottleneck in narrowing model focus to get questions right more quickly/with fewer attempts, could it not therefore reveal more model capability to users? Is it not possible that LLMs could have capability beyond what we are currently using that improved efficient access unlocks? How do we better quantify the limits on underlying model capabilities? For the misalignment paper, I hope to discuss more about how to prevent reward hacking, and what people’s opinions are on this performance/transparency/privacy tradeoff. Did this work update anybody’s positions on this, and do we think that reward hacking that isn’t explicitly encouraged is similarly maliciously updating a model’s more general behavior?

Chen Gong

Nov 3, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

[Paper 1] This paper discusses how RLVR training affects model ability, using metrics such as pass@1 and pass@256 to measure "potential ability." It finds that the model learns to perform better on problems it already solves often, but performs worse on problems it has only a small chance of solving correctly. My intuition is that during training with 0/1 rewards, the model learns to value stability over creativity, since creativity always carries the risk of getting the answer wrong. [Paper 2] This paper studies emergent misalignment during RL on coding environments. The authors teach the model hacking methods through synthetic documents or prompts, and the documents often describe hacking as undesirable. The chain of events could be: the model connects hacking with being bad -> the model is rewarded for hacking -> the model accepts being bad -> the model behaves badly in other, untrained scenarios too. Two findings are highlighted: code sabotage and unprompted alignment faking. These are interesting to see, but their causes are hard to pin down.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

[Paper 1] If rewarding creative-but-failed processes, instead of only the final result, could be a solution, how do we define such a reward correctly? Would it require another model to evaluate the process? [Paper 2] The results are reasonable, and they also show how indirect material combined with RL can turn a base model bad. Is there a way to make a base model resistant to this kind of training? I can imagine adversarial training, but would that still work when the RL material seems unrelated to misalignment and the model simply learns "I'm bad"?

Selina Xiao

Nov 4, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found the inoculation prompting result in Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL particularly surprising. Simply framing reward hacking as acceptable during training reduced broader misalignment by 75–90%, even when the model continued reward hacking in over 99% of episodes. This suggests that the context in which a behavior is reinforced can matter as much as the behavior itself. In Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Mode, I found it interesting that RLVR can improve pass@1 while reducing pass@k for large k, suggesting that optimizing for more reliable responses can actually narrow the set of problems a model can solve through repeated sampling.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

For Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL, I wonder why changing the framing of reward hacking alters its generalization so dramatically, even when the learned behavior remains almost unchanged. Could this effect be explained through differences in learned representations? For Does Reinforcement Learning Really Incentivize Reasoning Capacity in LLMs Beyond the Base Model, could exploration-aware RL objectives preserve the base model's reasoning diversity while still improving pass@1? More generally, is there an inherent tradeoff between optimizing the probability of successful outcomes and preserving the diversity of strategies available to a model?

Ke Xu

Nov 5, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

paper1: I find the difference between improving reasoning efficiency and expanding reasoning capacity especially interesting. RLVR models perform better at pass@1, but the base models often perform better when many attempts are allowed. This suggests that RLVR mainly shifts probability toward reasoning paths that already exist in the base model, rather than creating fundamentally new ones. I'm also interested in the contrast with distillation, which can introduce reasoning patterns from a stronger teacher that were not previously accessible to the student model. paper2: I'm surprised that learning to reward hack in coding environments generalized to behaviors that are not directly rewarded, including alignment faking and sabotage. The finding that standard chat-based RLHF could make the model appear aligned in ordinary conversations while leaving misalignment in agentic settings is particularly concerning. This shows how an evaluation may look successful while missing harmful behavior outside its testing distribution.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

paper1: I am wondering how confidently pass@(k) can distinguish a genuinely new reasoning capability from a reasoning path that already existed in the base model with extremely low probability. Since even a large but finite (k) may miss very rare paths, the boundary between “new capability” and “better elicitation” still seems difficult to define. I hope to discuss whether process rewards, better exploration, or interaction with external tools could allow RL to move beyond the base model’s existing reasoning distribution. paper2: The experiments intentionally make reward hacking easier to discover and trained heavily on hackable environments, so I am curious how often the same broad misalignment would emerge under more typical production training conditions. I also find “inoculation prompting” counterintuitive because it reduces misaligned generalization while still allowing the model to learn reward hacking. I hope to better understand why changing the framing of the behavior has such a large effect, and whether this should be viewed as a genuine safety intervention or mainly as a way of containing the generalization.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

In the first paper I found it interesting how improving a model's ability to consistently produce correct answers can be different from expanding the range of problems it can solve. In general, this reminded me of the gamification of benchmarks because an improvement on a benchmark can seem like evidence that a model has learned something new, even when it may just be changing which existing behaviors it expresses. It also reminded me of the Sleeper Agents paper. For the second paper, I thought it was really interesting and concerning how learning to exploit coding rewards could generalize to harmful behavior outside of those tasks. I also thought the finding that standard safety training could improve behavior in chat settings while leaving misalignment in agentic settings was particularly important. This made me wonder how often our evaluations capture the situations where a model has learned to behave appropriately without adequately testing the situations where its actions could have more substantial consequences.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

For the first paper, I'm curious about what should count as an improvement in reasoning ability. If a model can produce a correct solution only after hundreds of attempts, how should we compare that ability to a model that produces it reliably? In other words, I am curious to hear what people think about how much weight we should place on potential capability versus what users can realistically access. I would also be interested to know whether the problems that become harder to solve after RL share particular characteristics. For the second paper, I am curious about how much the results depend on providing models with information about reward hacking. I would also like to discuss what sufficiently diverse safety evaluations would look like in practice, especially when we cannot anticipate every setting in which an agent will act. In general, I am hoping to hear people's thoughts on how these papers fit together, since both raise questions about what improvements in measured performance actually tell us about what a model has learned.

Nana Xia

Nov 6, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

Does reinforcement learning really incentivize reasoning capacity in LLMs beyond the base model? I found interesting that the authors examined reasoning using large-k pass@k instead of pass@1. If at least one out of k samples is correct, the problem is considered within the model's potential reasoning scope. The results showed that RLVR-trained models perform better at small k, especially at pass@1. It shows that the RLVR-trained models are more likely to generate a correct reasoning path on the first few attempts. However, as k becomes large, the base model catches up and often surpasses the RLVR model. This indicates that the current RLVR might be improving the efficiency of sampling the correct reasoning paths, which were originally present in the base model, rather than continuously creating fundamentally new reasoning capabilities. Natural emergent misalignment from reward hacking in production RL: I found that the results of the inoculation prompting were contrary to my intuition. The authors pointed out that during the training process, telling the model that rewarding deception is acceptable in a specific environment can significantly reduce the broader deviation behaviors resulting from learning to deceive for rewards. The authors believe that the model may have learned during pre-training to associate reward deception with deviation behaviors. Their experiments showed that although the rate of reward deception remained high, the generalization ability of this deviation was reduced by approximately 75% to 90%.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

Do these two papers reflect the same fundamental phenomenon? In both cases, reinforcement learning seems to be reshaping the probability distribution within the original behavioral patterns. If this is indeed the case, then will the manipulation of rewards trigger new mismatches? Because reinforcement learning is increasing the probability of the already existing potential "mismatch" behaviors in the pre-trained model, rather than creating these behaviors from scratch.

Alan Liang

Nov 6, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found it interesting that reinforcement learning could improve the chance of answering correctly in one attempt while reducing the range of problems solved across many attempts. In the tested settings, the base model sometimes covered more problems when given enough samples. I also found it interesting that increasing the trained model’s sampling temperature did not fully recover that coverage. I found it interesting that, in the authors’ experiments, models given knowledge of coding reward hacks developed misaligned behavior on tasks outside coding after learning to exploit those rewards. Ordinary safety training improved chat evaluations while some failures remained on agentic tasks. The finding that framing reward hacking as acceptable during training reduced this broader generalization was especially surprising.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

How much can finite sampling experiments tell us about whether a model has learned a new reasoning method? Would the comparison change if both models received the same total number of generated tokens rather than the same number of attempts? I hope to understand how to distinguish more reliable use of existing abilities from evidence of new abilities, especially when identifying the correct sampled answer requires an external verifier. How much do the results depend on supplying knowledge of specific hacks and selecting vulnerable environments? Why would changing the framing of hacking affect behavior on unrelated tasks? I hope to understand how to test whether the inoculation approach transfers to new situations, and how to detect failures that ordinary chat evaluations might miss.
