Performative prediction is a super interesting idea! There is a concept in macroeconomics called "forward guidance" (I think this idea is implicitly alluded to in the paper) where a financial institution like the Federal Reserve will hint at what it will do in the future. In theory, this can have a large effect on the economy, even though the financial institution has not technically made a clear decision yet. From my understanding, RRM only converges if the loss is convex in the parameter $\theta$, which (again from my understanding) is not necessarily true in general. 1. In practice, does RRM converge for non-convex loss functions? 2. In theory, are there classes of not-necessarily-convex loss functions (for example PL loss functions) where we can obtain weak convergence guarantees for RRM?

Selina Xiao

Sep 22, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

What I found most interesting is the distinction between performative stability and performative optimality. A model can be stable in the sense that retraining on the distribution created by its own deployment returns the same model, while still being suboptimal in terms of the actual post-deployment risk. This makes repeated retraining look less like a procedure for solving the true objective and more like a fixed-point dynamic whose solution may or may not be desirable. I also liked the paper's decomposition of performative risk into learning and steering. Once the deployed model changes the data distribution, performance can improve either because the model predicts the current population better or because it shifts the population into a distribution that is easier or more profitable to predict. That distinction makes the optimization problem fundamentally different from standard supervised learning and gives a concrete way to think about feedback effects rather than treating them as generic distribution shift.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I had is whether performative stability is actually a sufficient objective. Since stable points need not be performatively optimal, it would be useful to characterize conditions that bound the gap between the two, rather than only proving that retraining converges. I was also interested in the fact that performative risk can become nonconvex even when the original loss is strongly convex. Instead of treating the resulting problem as a generic black-box optimization problem and moving directly to derivative-free or bandit methods, can properties of the distribution map be used to characterize the structure of this nonconvexity and design more specialized optimization methods?

Sriram Kadiyala

Sep 22, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

It was interesting to comprehend (because it is often taken for granted) that predictions actually change the environment, and that minimizing risk isn't just about fitting the data better, it's about actively altering the underlying data distribution to reach a lower-loss state. Thinking about recommendation algorithms as tools optimized to steer human behavior rather than just passively predict it makes me look at them in a completely different way. The way the authors formalized "Performative Power" to connect statistical learning directly to antitrust law is super clever, in my opinion. The fact that a monopoly maximizes performative power, but having two competing platforms offering perfect substitutes crashes that power down to basically zero, really shows how well their definition tracks standard economic concepts of competition.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

The authors mention that continuously retraining a model converges to a "performatively stable" point, which they compare to an echo chamber where the data just stops challenging the model's beliefs. There is a huge asymmetry here in my opinion: stability is cheap to certify (you just retrain and check that you didn't improve), but optimality isn't. So in a live production environment, how do teams actually estimate how much performance they're leaving on the table? The paper's own solution is to use bandits for global exploration, but that I think requires actively deploying models you expect to be worse, which seems like a very tough sell in industry. Would love to learn this from the discussion.
