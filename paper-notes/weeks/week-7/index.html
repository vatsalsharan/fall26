Anu Soneye

Sep 22, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

○ The paper articulated a concern I had for some time about alignment methods for LLMs: because the input space is so vast, passing a bias benchmark does not guarantee that a model is genuinely free from bias. This concern was echoed in a paper by Amazon researchers, who made an existing benchmark more challenging by lengthening model responses, thereby creating more opportunities for biases to emerge. The machine learning community often addresses the limitations of benchmarks by making them increasingly difficult, but I wonder whether there is a fundamentally better approach to evaluating bias. ○ The parallel methods from human psychology created to reveal implicit bias in LLMs was also pretty interesting For humans, it makes sense to evaluate how they behave in practice rather than rely on explicit tests, which may produce inaccurate results because of "social desirability" bias.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

○ I appreciated that the authors acknowledged several concerns I had while reading, particularly in the Discussion section. For example, they noted the lack of mechanistic interpretation needed to identify the causal mechanisms underlying a model’s biased behavior. Nevertheless, I remain unconvinced that these tests provide a useful diagnostic tool. Even for relatively small DNNs, machine-learning experts struggle to determine which internal decisions produced biased outputs based solely on counterexamples. Moreover, the space of possible counterexamples is prohibitively large. ○ The authors also argue that we should not “desire a model that exhibits no bias,” since this could indicate a “race-blind” model incapable of performing important tasks, such as recognizing stereotypes. If eliminating all measured bias is not the objective, what constitutes an appropriate bias threshold? Perhaps the acceptable threshold should depend on the model’s intended task and deployment context. Are extrinsic measurement methods necessarily better than intrinsic?

Selina Xiao

Sep 24, 2026

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

What I found most interesting is that the paper does not stop at showing that aligned models still produce stereotypical associations, but tries to connect those associations to actual downstream decisions. In GPT-4, prompt-based word association bias predicts relative decision bias more strongly than embedding-based bias, and each unit increase in word association bias corresponds to about a 2.68-fold increase in the odds of a discriminatory decision. I also found the relative versus absolute comparison particularly interesting. GPT-4 looks much less biased when evaluating one person independently, but the bias becomes much more visible when it has to choose between two candidates. This suggests that standard benchmarks may partly measure whether a model has learned how to give an explicitly acceptable answer, rather than whether the underlying associations that affect comparative decisions have actually changed.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I had is how much of the reported relationship between word association bias and decision bias reflects a genuinely shared underlying mechanism, rather than similarity in how the two tests are constructed. The authors intentionally match the decision tasks to the word-association tasks and, for the correlation analysis, even place both tasks consecutively in the same prompt, which makes the relationship easier to measure but may also inflate it. The paper itself acknowledges that this design may limit ecological validity. I would also want to know whether the finding that larger models show stronger word association bias is really a model-size effect. The eight models differ not only in parameter count, but also in architecture, training data, and alignment procedure, so a cleaner experiment would compare checkpoints of the same model family before and after alignment and across scales.

Nana Xia

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

What I found most interesting is that relative decisions reveal more bias than absolute decisions. When the model is asked a direct question, such as whether women should study science, it tends to give a fair answer. However, when it is asked to assign science and humanities to a man and a woman, it is more likely to follow traditional gender stereotypes. I found this distinction interesting because expressing a fairness principle is different from applying it when making decisions between people. In real-world applications such as hiring or allocating opportunities, the model often has to compare candidates rather than evaluate each person independently. This made me realize that fairness becomes more challenging when the model needs to distribute opportunities among different people, even if it appears unbiased when answering straightforward questions about equality.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

One question I have is what the actual goal of debiasing should be. The authors mention that we do not necessarily want a model with no bias, since a model may need to recognize existing stereotypes in order to identify discrimination. This makes me wonder whether we should focus more on reducing discriminatory decisions rather than trying to eliminate all stereotypical associations. If some associations remain in the model, how can we prevent them from influencing decisions in situations such as hiring or allocating opportunities?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

The authors briefly state in their discussion that it is not clear how to compare the resulting scores for human implicit bias and bias measured by the LLM Word Association Test, which I found interesting. This stuck out to me specifically because our lab works with a lot of psychological measures, and we frequently encounter challenges with maintaining the psychological validity of measures as they are incorporated into our studies (e.g. if you so much as change a word, the validity of the measure and your results could come into question). So although the authors pose their measures as being based off of psychology literature, I struggle to see how they could make a comparison between the human measure and the LLM-specific measure without encountering some resistance. Reliability and validity of the measures would need to be established on LLMs, correlation between LLM and human measures should be tested... Ultimately, this is not a trivial challenge, but it is not one without starting points found in psych literature. Reducing this to “It is not clear how to compare the resulting scores.” was very entertaining to me because of how much it obscures.

I wonder if smaller LLMs have less bias because they can't pick up on nuanced details the way bigger LLMs can, since they have fewer parameters? Or, for lack of a better word, are they unbiased because they are less capable? I hope to discuss whether we should use tests like this during the training process itself to identify and fix some of these implicit biases.

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I found the distinction between explicit and implicit bias in LLMs particularly interesting. Models that appear largely unbiased on standard benchmarks can still exhibit stereotypical associations when evaluated through more subtle word-association and relative-decision tasks. I also found the use of methods adapted from psychology interesting because they allow researchers to study these associations purely through observable model behavior, even when the model is proprietary and its internal representations are inaccessible. What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

I would be interested in discussing what exactly these behavioral associations tell us about an LLM, and where the analogy to human implicit bias breaks down. More broadly, how can we distinguish a meaningful latent bias that is likely to affect downstream decisions from an association that can be elicited experimentally but has little practical behavioral consequence?

What are things you found interesting about the papers for this class?

I thought it was really interesting how the paper distinguishes between a model explicitly producing biased outputs and still having biased associations internally. In particular, I found it interesting that models can learn not to express certain stereotypes without actually eliminating the associations that lead to them in the first place. This reminded me a lot of how alignment methods can sometimes change the behavior we observe without necessarily changing what the model has learned, which seems especially important when we think about evaluating models for fairness. If we mostly evaluate bias based on model outputs, it seems possible that we could conclude that a model has been successfully debiased when the underlying associations are still there and could potentially show up under different prompts or settings. I also thought this was interesting from an interpretability perspective, since it raises the question of whether we should be looking beyond outputs when trying to understand whether a model is actually behaving fairly.

What questions do you have about the papers for this class? What do you hope to learn from the discussion?

I am curious about what the practical implications of these hidden biased associations are. If a model has biased internal associations but consistently avoids expressing them in its outputs, is that necessarily harmful, or does the concern mainly come from the possibility that those associations could affect behavior in settings that weren't tested? I thought about the same question in the context of humans, and I think people would generally agree that a racist person who hides that they are racist is still problematic. I am also curious about what this means for current debiasing and alignment methods more generally; are we mostly teaching models not to express bias rather than actually changing the representations that produce it? I would be interested to hear what people think a good standard for calling a model "unbiased" should be, especially if behavioral evaluations and internal representations can give us different answers. Lastly, I am curious to hear what people think in general about bias in AI; is our goal to eliminate it considering training data reflect a biased society? If so, how, and who will decide what unbiased means?
