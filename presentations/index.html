Presentation rubric

Talks should run for about 20 minutes, followed by questions and discussion. Use these criteria to decide what deserves space in your talk.

5 points

Plan this early

Check in with the TA before your presentation.

Meet a few days before your talk to review your slides and receive feedback. No check-in earns 0 points; completing it earns 5 points.

3 pts

Build a talk, not a paper summary

Advice for a strong presentation

Learning to present research clearly is one of the most important skills to develop in a Ph.D. or research career. It takes time, preparation, feedback, and a clear point of view.

01

Practice once

If possible, give one practice talk to a friend or classmate. Use their feedback to refine the talk before class.

02

Choose one or two ideas

You are not expected to cover everything. Focus on a few main results, insights, or takeaways and connect them into one clear story.

03

Use notation selectively

A notation-heavy paper does not require a notation-heavy talk. Use the simplest clean setting that communicates the idea, even when that means sacrificing some generality or rigor.

04

Read strategically

It is fine to skim some sections and study the main parts carefully. For a long paper, roughly 20–30 well-chosen pages can be enough to understand and present its key ideas.

05

Make the takeaway visible

Help the audience see why each slide matters. Prefer clear language and key insights over extra details and jargon.

06

Connect back to class

Wherever possible, relate the paper’s questions, methods, or conclusions to material already covered in the course.
